#### Scene 3 · 0:45 to 3:00 · Closed vs open

**[two-sided map]** Every model you'll use falls into one of two worlds.

**["Closed garden"]** On this side is the closed garden. These are models like ChatGPT from OpenAI and Claude from Anthropic. The model lives on the company's servers. You can't download it. You use it through their app, or through an **API**, which is a way for your code to send it a message and get a reply. You pay per use, usually per token.

**[hold on closed side]** The big advantages: they're usually the most capable models, there's nothing to set up, and they scale instantly. The trade-offs: you pay for every request, your data goes to someone else's servers, and the company decides when a model changes or retires.

**["Open city"]** On this side is the open city. These are **open-weight** models. Remember the weights, the billions of dials from Day 3? With open models, the company publishes those weights, and you can download them and run the model yourself: on your laptop, on your own server, anywhere.

**[hold on open side]** The advantages here: privacy, because your data never has to leave your machine; control, because nobody can change the model on you; and no per-token bill, though you do pay for the hardware. The trade-off is that you do more of the work yourself.

**[both sides]** Neither side is better. They're different tools, and in this course, you'll use both.

#### Scene 4 · 3:00 to 5:30 · OpenAI and ChatGPT

**[OpenAI tier pyramid]** Let's start with OpenAI, the company that started the chatbot boom. Like most providers, they don't have one model. They have a family.

**[flagship at the top]** At the top is the flagship: the most capable, and the most expensive. Below it are smaller, faster and much cheaper versions. A lot of real apps use the smaller ones, because they're good enough and cost a fraction of the price.

**[reasoning badge]** You'll also see **reasoning** models. These take extra time to think through a problem step by step before answering. They're great for maths, coding and tricky logic, but slower and pricier, so you don't need them for simple tasks.

**[model names card]** Model names and versions change every few months, so I won't promise you exact names here. The card on screen shows the current line-up, and the OpenAI models page, linked below, always has the latest list.

**[split card: ChatGPT app vs OpenAI API]** Now, one point that confuses almost everyone. **ChatGPT** is an app, made for people. You chat in a browser or on your phone, and you might pay a monthly subscription. The **OpenAI API** is for developers. Your code talks to the models directly, and you pay per token. They're separate products with separate billing. A ChatGPT subscription doesn't give you API credit. From Day 9, we'll be using the API.

#### Scene 5 · 5:30 to 7:45 · Anthropic and Claude

**[Claude tier pyramid]** Next, Anthropic, who make Claude. Their family is easy to remember, because it's named after creative works, from big to small: an opus, a sonnet and a haiku.

**[Opus]** At the top is **Opus**, the most capable. It's for the hardest work: complex reasoning, long documents, big coding tasks.

**[Sonnet]** In the middle is **Sonnet**, the balanced one. It's smart, fast and reasonably priced, and it's what a lot of people use day to day.

**[Haiku]** And at the bottom is **Haiku**, the fastest and cheapest. It's perfect for quick, high-volume jobs like sorting messages or pulling details out of text.

**[version card]** Each of these gets updated with new version numbers over time. Again, the exact versions are on the card, and the Anthropic models page, linked below, is always the source of truth.

**[split card: Claude app vs Claude API]** And it's the same split as OpenAI. The **Claude app** is for chatting, with free and paid plans. The **Claude API**, through the Anthropic console, is for developers, billed per token. Claude is especially known for writing, for coding, and for handling very long documents.

**[hold]** One handy tip: the same pattern of big, medium and small shows up at almost every provider. So when you see a new model family, ask one question: which one is the big one, and which one is the cheap, fast one?

#### Scene 6 · 7:45 to 9:45 · Open-weight families

**[shelf of labeled boxes]** Now, the open city. Here are the main families you'll meet.

**[Llama]** **Llama**, from Meta, one of the most popular open families, with a huge community around it.

**[Mistral]** **Mistral**, from a French company, known for small, efficient models.

**[Qwen]** **Qwen**, from Alibaba, very strong at many languages and at coding.

**[Gemma]** **Gemma**, from Google, designed to be small enough to run on everyday devices.

**[DeepSeek]** And **DeepSeek**, known for strong reasoning models at a very low cost.

**[size tags: 8B, 70B]** Look at the tags on each box. "8B" means eight billion parameters, eight billion dials. "70B" means seventy billion. Bigger usually means smarter, but it also needs much more memory to run. On Day 8, I'll show you how to tell what your laptop can handle.

**[Hugging Face library]** And where do you find all of these? The **Hugging Face Hub**. Think of it as a giant library of AI models, with hundreds of thousands of them, free to browse and download.

**[license badge flips]** One last thing before you download anything: check the **license**. "Open weights" means you can download and use the model, but there may be rules, like limits on commercial use. Truly "open source" usually means fewer restrictions, and sometimes the training code and data too. If you're building something for a business, always read the license on the model page.

#### Scene 7 · 9:45 to 11:15 · How to choose

**[balance scale]** So how do you choose? Weigh four things.

**[Quality]** **Quality**: how good does the answer need to be? A legal summary needs more than a joke generator.

**[Cost, price tag slides]** **Cost**: prices are quoted per million tokens, and the gap between the biggest and the smallest models can be huge, often ten times or more.

**[Speed]** **Speed**: does someone need the answer in under a second, or can it take a minute?

**[Privacy]** And **privacy**: can this data leave your building at all? If not, an open model on your own machine might be the only option.

**[leaderboard with caution]** You'll see leaderboards like LMArena ranking models against each other. They're useful, but the only test that really matters is your own. My rule of thumb: start with a mid-sized model, test it on twenty real examples of your task, and only move up if it isn't good enough, or down if it's more than good enough.
