#### Scene 1 · 0:00 to 0:30 · Cold open hook

**[programmer writing if-statements]** Imagine you're asked to build a spam filter. So you start writing rules. If the email says "you've won", it's spam. If it says "free money", spam. If it has five exclamation marks... spam.

**[list grows off-screen]** But the scammers change their words every day, and your list of rules just keeps growing and breaking.

**[model draws a boundary]** Now, what if you didn't write the rules at all? What if the computer looked at the examples and wrote the rules itself?

#### Scene 2 · 0:30 to 0:45 · Journey map

**[Token at stop 2]** Welcome back to Day 2 of *AI Fundamentals in 15 Days*. Yesterday we drew the big map of AI. Today we zoom into one circle, machine learning, and see how machines actually learn.

#### Scene 3 · 0:45 to 3:00 · Rules vs data

**[split screen appears]** Here's the whole idea of machine learning in one picture.

**[left side: Rules + Data -> Answers]** On the left is traditional programming. A person writes the rules, the computer takes in data, and out come answers. A calculator works like this. A tax form works like this. You give it the rules, and it follows them perfectly.

**[right side: Data + Answers -> Rules]** On the right is machine learning, and notice what's changed. We give the computer the data *and* the answers. Thousands of emails, each one already marked spam or not spam. And the computer works out the rules on its own.

**[boxes swap]** That's the flip. In normal programming, rules go in. In machine learning, rules come *out*.

**[hold on the swap]** Why does this matter? Because some rules are impossible for people to write down. Try writing the rules for recognizing your mum's face, or for telling a happy review from a sarcastic one. You know it when you see it, but you can't explain it step by step. Machine learning lets us skip that step. We show examples, and the computer finds the pattern.

**[hold]** The catch is that the rules it finds are only as good as the examples we give it. Keep that in mind. We'll come back to it at the end.

#### Scene 4 · 3:00 to 5:00 · Features, labels, model

**[house icon splits into tags]** Let's make this concrete with a house. Say we want to predict the price of a house.

**[size, rooms, location tags]** First, we describe each house with a few facts: its size, the number of rooms, its location. In machine learning these facts are called **features**. They're the inputs.

**[price tag pops out]** The price is what we want to predict. When we already know the price for past sales, that known answer is called the **label**.

**[tags flow into the model box]** The **model** sits in the middle. It's the thing that learns how features turn into a price.

**[scatter plot appears]** Here's what learning looks like. Each dot is a house that's already been sold: its size along the bottom, its price going up.

**[line wobbles and fits]** The model starts with a bad guess, this line. Then it checks how far off it is from the real prices, adjusts a little, and checks again. Step by step, the line settles into the pattern. That process is called **training**.

**[line settles]** Once it's trained, we can give it a brand-new house it's never seen, and it predicts a price. Using a trained model like this is called **inference**. Training is the learning. Inference is the using. You'll hear both words for the rest of this course.

#### Scene 5 · 5:00 to 7:15 · Three ways to learn

**[three doors]** Machines can learn in three main ways. Let's open each door.

**[door 1: cards sorted into cat and dog bins]** Door one is **supervised learning**. Every example comes with the right answer, like a teacher marking your homework. These photos are cats, these are dogs. The model learns to sort new photos into the right bin. Spam filters and our house prices are supervised learning.

**[door 2: dots cluster]** Door two is **unsupervised learning**. Here there are no answers at all. We just hand the model a pile of data and ask: what groups do you see? A shop might use it to discover that its customers fall into, say, bargain hunters, weekend shoppers and big spenders, without anyone defining those groups first.

**[door 3: robot in a maze]** Door three is **reinforcement learning**. Nobody gives the right answer. The model tries things, and gets a reward when it does well. This little robot tries a path, hits a wall, tries again, and collects a star when it gets closer to the exit. Over thousands of tries, it learns the best route. That's how AI learned to beat people at games like Go.

**[reward counter climbs]** And here's a fun fact: chatbots like ChatGPT and Claude use all three. They first learn patterns from huge amounts of text without labels. Then they're shown good example answers. And finally they're polished with rewards based on which answers people preferred.

#### Scene 6 · 7:15 to 8:45 · Train/test split and overfitting

**[deck splits 80/20]** How do we know if a model actually learned something? We hold some data back. Usually around eighty percent goes into training, and twenty percent is hidden away for testing. The model never sees the test data while it's learning.

**[hold on Test pile]** It's like a teacher keeping some exam questions secret. If you only memorized the practice questions, you'll struggle on the real exam.

**[wiggly red curve]** And that's exactly what this red curve did. It bends to pass through every single training point. Perfect score on practice. But it memorized the noise, so on new data it falls apart. That's called **overfitting**.

**[too-straight line]** This straight line has the opposite problem. It's too simple to capture the pattern at all. That's **underfitting**.

**[smooth green curve]** And this one is just right. It follows the overall trend without chasing every point. That's the model that will do well on data it's never seen, which is the whole point.

#### Scene 7 · 8:45 to 9:30 · Garbage in, garbage out

**[messy data into funnel]** Remember the catch from earlier? A model learns whatever is in its data, including the mistakes. If a hiring model is trained on years of biased decisions, it learns that bias, and repeats it.

**[clean data, fair prediction]** Clean, balanced data gives you a fairer, more useful model. There's an old saying for this: garbage in, garbage out.

#### Scene 8 · 9:30 to 11:30 · Train your first model

**[Teachable Machine image project]** Now let's train a real model, right in your browser. Open Teachable Machine and choose **Image Project**, then **Standard image model**.

**[callout: add class 1]** You'll see two boxes called classes. Rename the first one to something like "Me".

**[callout: add class 2]** Rename the second one, maybe "Coffee mug". Pick anything you have nearby.

**[callout: record samples]** Click **Webcam**, then hold the record button to capture samples. Get around fifty to a hundred pictures for each class, and move around a little, so the model sees different angles and lighting. Those pictures are your labeled training data. Supervised learning, just like door one.

**[callout: Train]** Now click **Train Model**. Keep the tab open. In under a minute, it's finished.

**[callout: Preview]** Look at the preview on the right. Show it your face, then the mug, and watch the confidence bars move. That's inference, live.

**[hold on preview]** Now try to break it. Show it something it's never seen, like your hand or a different cup. What does it guess? That's your model meeting test data, and you'll often find it's confidently wrong. Add more varied samples, retrain, and see if it improves. That loop of test, fix and retrain is what machine learning engineers do every day.
